## What it is
The Badlands GTX Long is KLIM's premium all-weather adventure glove for serious winter and wet-condition riding. At $269.99 (Gen 2, SKU 3923-001), it sits at the top of KLIM's glove range and earns it: Gore-Tex + Gore Grip laminated to goat leather (the membrane is bonded to the outer — it won't wet out in sustained rain), 60g 3M Thinsulate backhand insulation, a polycarbonate hard knuckle guard backed with XRD foam, a neoprene gasket that seals the gauntlet around the wrist, and dual visor wipers — one for water (left), one for ice (right). Set-and-forget straps at the wrist and forearm complete the sealed system.

Motolegends describe the Gen 2 as "hugely improved in tactile feel and dexterity" over the previous generation and note the Gore Grip laminate as the glove "that won't wet out" — a pointed distinction from waterproof gloves with a loose inner liner that eventually saturates.

This is an adventure touring rider's serious winter solution. At this price, the customer should know exactly what they're getting: a fully sealed, properly insulated, hard-shell-protected glove that KLIM have engineered to be worn year-round in the worst conditions.

## Top selling points

1. **Gore Grip laminated membrane — it won't wet out.** The membrane is bonded to the outer leather, not floating inside as a loose liner. A loose-liner waterproof glove gradually allows moisture to seep between the liner and the outer; eventually the liner wets out from the inside. The Gore Grip laminate eliminates this. Motolegends specifically flag this in their editorial: the Badlands GTX Long is "a laminated Gore-Grip membrane that means this is a glove that won't wet out." For a customer who's been burned by waterproof gloves that eventually let them down, this is the compelling answer.

2. **Neoprene gasket — the wrist is actually sealed.** Most waterproof gauntlet gloves rely on the gauntlet cuff to keep water out of the sleeve interface, but this depends on how tightly the jacket sleeve fits against the glove. The neoprene gasket inside the Badlands GTX Long creates a positive seal at the wrist — the cuff doesn't rely on friction fit. In persistent rain or cold water spray at motorway speeds, this distinction matters.

3. **Dual visor wipers — water on the left, ice on the right.** A detail that sounds minor until you're riding in freezing rain at 70mph and your visor is icing. The left index finger clears water; the right clears ice. No other glove in the KLIM range includes this. Adventure and touring riders who ride year-round will appreciate it; it's a working feature, not a gimmick.

4. **Hard polycarbonate knuckle + XRD foam.** The Badlands GTX Long uses a hard polycarbonate shell over XRD foam — a step up from the softer rubber/foam protectors in some competing winter gloves. CE Level 1 KP certified. Hard-shell protection matters when falling at ADV speeds on rough terrain; XRD's non-Newtonian foam provides energy absorption beneath the hard shell.

5. **Set-and-forget dual-strap closure — it stays done up.** Two closure straps: one at the wrist, one at the forearm. KLIM design them to be adjusted once and left — no fiddling at the side of the road in the rain. Combined with the neoprene gasket, this gives a fully sealed system that riders can rely on all day.

6. **Gen 2 — meaningfully better than Gen 1.** Motolegends explicitly note "huge improvements in tactile feel and dexterity" in the Gen 2. If a customer previously tried the original Badlands GTX Long and dismissed it, the Gen 2 (3923-001) is a substantially better glove.

## Common questions customers might ask + good answers

**"How is this different from the Vanguard GTX Long?"**
Both have 60g Thinsulate, Gore-Tex waterproofing, and CE Level 1 KP. The Badlands GTX Long adds: a polycarbonate hard-shell knuckle guard (Vanguard has a softer protector), a neoprene gasket that physically seals the wrist (Vanguard relies on the gauntlet cuff fit), dual visor wipers (water and ice vs single wiper), ceramic print armour overlays, and biomotion reflective patterning. It's also $269.99 vs the Vanguard Long's $179.99 — a meaningful premium. For a road touring rider, the Vanguard Long is well equipped; for an adventure rider doing long-distance miles in proper winter conditions, the Badlands GTX Long's hard shell and sealed wrist are worth the difference.

**"Why does the glove have TWO visor wipers?"**
One for water, one for ice — they're different materials. The left-hand index finger wipes water off a wet visor; the right-hand index finger scrapes ice or frost. Relevant in early morning starts, hill descents in cold air, or freezing rain. On a dedicated winter glove at $269.99, it's an intentional and useful feature.

**"What's Gore Grip and why does it matter?"**
Gore Grip is the Gore-Tex construction where the waterproof membrane is laminated (bonded) directly to the outer leather. The alternative — a loose inner sock liner with a separate waterproof membrane — eventually allows moisture to creep between the outer and the liner through normal wear and cuff opening/closing. With the laminated Gore Grip construction, there's nowhere for water to infiltrate from the outside. It also means the glove fits like leather, not like a waterproof liner inside a leather shell — the dexterity is better.

**"What's the neoprene gasket?"**
Inside the gauntlet cuff, there's a neoprene band that seals around the wrist when the glove is fully on. When the two straps are cinched, the gasket compresses gently against the wrist — the same principle as a wetsuit collar. It prevents water from being driven up the sleeve interface when rain hits the wrist zone at speed. Most gauntlet gloves don't have this; they rely only on the cuff flap sitting over the jacket sleeve.

**"Is this warm enough for riding in winter?"**
60g Thinsulate on the backhand is meaningful insulation for 0°C–10°C commuting or touring. Below 0°C for extended periods, riders may want grip heaters on the bars. The neoprene gasket and sealed gauntlet prevent cold air infiltration at the wrist, which is typically the biggest source of cold ingress in gloves that otherwise perform well.
